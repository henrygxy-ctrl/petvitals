## Quick Answer

The most common household poisons for pets are human medications, toxic foods, plants, cleaning chemicals, antifreeze, and rodenticides. For dogs, high-risk items include [xylitol](/toxicity/xylitol), [grapes and raisins](/toxicity/grapes), [chocolate](/toxicity/chocolate), [onions](/toxicity/onions), [garlic](/toxicity/garlic), [macadamia nuts](/toxicity/macadamia-nuts), NSAIDs, and antifreeze. For cats, the most urgent household risks include [lilies](/toxicity/lilies), acetaminophen, NSAIDs, rodenticides, antifreeze, and concentrated cleaners.

If your pet may have eaten a toxin, do not wait for symptoms. Call your veterinarian, an emergency clinic, ASPCA Animal Poison Control, or Pet Poison Helpline, and keep the package or plant photo with you.

## Kitchen

### 1. Xylitol (Birch Sugar)

Xylitol is found in sugar-free gum, mints, candies, baked goods, some peanut butter, supplements, and dental products. It can cause a rapid, severe blood sugar drop in dogs. Higher exposures may also trigger liver injury.

**Watch for:** vomiting, weakness, wobbling, collapse, tremors, or seizures. Signs can begin quickly, but liver-related problems may develop later.

**What to do:** go straight to emergency care or call poison control. Do not wait to see whether your dog "seems fine." Read the full [xylitol toxicity guide](/toxicity/xylitol) if you need the risk summary.

### 2. Grapes and Raisins

Grapes and raisins are dangerous because the toxic dose is unpredictable. Some dogs eat several with no obvious issue; others develop serious kidney injury after a small exposure. That uncertainty is why there is no safe household amount.

**Common sources:** trail mix, raisin bread, granola, cereal, baked goods, lunch boxes, and dropped snacks.

**What to do:** call your vet or poison control right away, especially if ingestion was recent. See our deeper guide to [grape and raisin poisoning in dogs](/toxicity/grapes).

### 3. Onions, Garlic, Chives, and Leeks

Allium foods can damage red blood cells and cause anemia. The risk applies to raw, cooked, powdered, dehydrated, and concentrated forms. Onion powder and garlic powder are easy to miss in soup, sauces, seasoning mixes, baby food, and leftovers.

Cats are often more sensitive than dogs, but dogs are also at risk, especially with repeated small exposures.

**Watch for:** vomiting, weakness, pale gums, fast breathing, dark urine, or collapse. Signs may be delayed.

Compare [onion toxicity](/toxicity/onions) and [garlic toxicity](/toxicity/garlic) before giving seasoned leftovers.

### 4. Chocolate and Macadamia Nuts

Chocolate toxicity depends on the pet's weight, chocolate type, and amount eaten. Dark chocolate, cocoa powder, and baking chocolate are more dangerous than milk chocolate. Symptoms may include vomiting, diarrhea, agitation, fast heart rate, tremors, seizures, or collapse.

Macadamia nuts can cause weakness, vomiting, tremors, and high body temperature in dogs. They are not usually the same emergency pattern as xylitol or antifreeze, but they still deserve a call to your vet.

Use the [chocolate toxicity guide](/toxicity/chocolate) and [macadamia nut toxicity page](/toxicity/macadamia-nuts) for item-specific next steps.

## Bathroom and Medicine Cabinet

### 5. Human NSAIDs: Ibuprofen and Naproxen

Never give a pet human pain relievers unless a veterinarian specifically tells you to. Ibuprofen and naproxen can cause stomach ulcers, kidney injury, neurologic signs, and life-threatening toxicity. Cats are especially vulnerable because they metabolize many drugs differently than people.

**Common exposure pattern:** a bottle gets chewed, a pill is dropped, or an owner gives a small human dose trying to help pain.

**What to do:** save the bottle, count missing tablets, and call a vet immediately. Review [ibuprofen](/toxicity/ibuprofen) and [naproxen](/toxicity/naproxen) for risk details.

### 6. Acetaminophen (Tylenol)

Acetaminophen is especially dangerous to cats and can also harm dogs. In cats, it can damage red blood cells and impair oxygen delivery. In dogs, it can injure the liver at higher exposures.

**Watch for:** facial swelling, brown or blue gums, weakness, vomiting, breathing trouble, or collapse.

**What to do:** emergency care immediately. The antidote works best when started early. See [acetaminophen toxicity](/toxicity/acetaminophen).

## Living Room and Plants

### 7. Lilies

True lilies are among the most serious cat toxins in the home. Leaves, petals, pollen, stems, and vase water can all be dangerous. Kidney injury can develop even when the first signs look mild.

**High-risk lilies:** Easter lily, tiger lily, Asiatic lily, daylily, stargazer lily, and other true lilies.

**What to do:** treat any cat exposure as an emergency. Remove the plant, bag a sample or take photos, and go to a veterinary clinic. Start with [lily toxicity in cats](/toxicity/lilies) and our article on [lily poisoning](/blog/lily-toxicity-cats).

### 8. Sago Palm

Sago palm is dangerous to both dogs and cats. The seeds contain the highest toxin concentration, but all parts are unsafe. Ingestion can cause vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, seizures, liver failure, and death.

This is one plant that should not be kept in a pet household, especially if you have a curious puppy or chewing cat. Read the [sago palm toxicity page](/toxicity/sago-palm) and our [sago palm pet guide](/blog/sago-palm-toxicity-pets).

## Garage, Yard, and Laundry Room

### 9. Antifreeze

Ethylene glycol antifreeze can taste sweet and cause rapid kidney failure. Early signs can look like drunkenness: stumbling, vomiting, sleepiness, increased thirst, or acting disoriented. Waiting for the "kidney stage" can make treatment much harder.

**What to do:** emergency care immediately. Bring the product container if you have it. See [antifreeze toxicity](/toxicity/antifreeze).

### 10. Rodenticides and Concentrated Cleaners

Rodenticides are not all the same. Anticoagulant baits can cause internal bleeding, bromethalin can cause neurologic signs, and cholecalciferol products can cause dangerous calcium changes. The exact active ingredient drives treatment, so the package matters.

Concentrated cleaners can irritate or burn the mouth, throat, stomach, skin, or airways. Bleach and ammonia are both household risks, and mixing products can create dangerous fumes.

**What to do:** do not induce vomiting unless a veterinarian or poison-control expert tells you to. Keep pets away from the area, ventilate if fumes are present, and call for guidance. Compare [rat poison](/toxicity/rat-poison), [bleach](/toxicity/bleach), and [ammonia](/toxicity/ammonia) risk pages.

If the exposure involved a known toxin, the absence of symptoms is not reassuring. Many treatments work best before signs become severe.

## What to Do If You Suspect Poisoning

1. Remove your pet from the substance.
2. Save the package, pill bottle, plant label, photo, or ingredient list.
3. Estimate the amount eaten and the time of exposure.
4. Call your veterinarian, emergency clinic, ASPCA Animal Poison Control, or Pet Poison Helpline.
5. Do not induce vomiting, give milk, give oil, or use home remedies unless a professional tells you to.
6. If told to go in, bring your pet and all evidence with you.

Treatment may involve decontamination, bloodwork, activated charcoal, IV fluids, antidotes, hospitalization, or surgery. ## Prevention Checklist

- Keep medications in closed cabinets, not purses or nightstands.
- Move gum, mints, candy, and supplements out of reach.
- Keep grapes, raisins, chocolate, and allium-heavy leftovers away from counters and trash.
- Do not keep lilies in a cat household.
- Remove sago palm from pet-accessible spaces.
- Store antifreeze, pesticides, fertilizers, and rodenticides behind closed doors.
- Use trash cans with locking lids.
- Choose cleaners from a pet-safe cleaning list, and let floors dry before pets return.
- Save poison-control numbers in your phone before you need them.
