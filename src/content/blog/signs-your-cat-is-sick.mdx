## Quick Answer

A cat may be sick if you notice a clear change in appetite, water intake, litter box habits, hiding, grooming, weight, breathing, voice, posture, or energy. Cats often hide discomfort, so the most useful warning sign is not one dramatic symptom - it is a change from your cat's normal routine.

Call a veterinarian promptly if your cat stops eating, hides for more than a day, strains in the litter box, vomits repeatedly, loses weight, breathes with effort, or seems weak. Go to emergency care immediately for open-mouth breathing, inability to urinate, collapse, pale gums, seizures, or known toxin exposure.

## Why Cats Hide Illness

Cats are both predators and prey animals. In the wild, showing weakness can make an animal more vulnerable, so cats often mask pain, fatigue, nausea, and fear. At home, this means a sick cat may simply become quieter, sleep somewhere unusual, stop jumping as high, or eat a little less.

That subtle pattern is why cat health problems are often found late. A cat does not need to cry, limp, or collapse to be sick. Small changes deserve attention when they are new, repeated, or out of character.

## 12 Warning Signs That Warrant Attention

### 1. Changes in Litter Box Habits

This is one of the most reliable early indicators. Urinating outside the box is not spite. Medical causes can include urinary tract inflammation, bladder stones, kidney disease, diabetes, arthritis, pain, or stress-related cystitis.

A cat straining in the box but producing little or no urine is an emergency, especially in male cats. Urinary blockage can become fatal quickly.

**Track:** urine clump size, frequency, blood, crying, accidents, and whether your cat repeatedly enters the box without producing urine.

### 2. Hiding More Than Usual

A cat who suddenly retreats under the bed, into closets, or behind furniture may be painful, nauseated, feverish, anxious, or weak. Hiding is more concerning when paired with low appetite, poor grooming, vomiting, breathing changes, or litter box changes.

If hiding persists beyond 24 hours, or if your cat will not come out to eat, call your veterinarian.

### 3. Appetite Changes

A reduced appetite for more than a day warrants a call. Cats are not small dogs; prolonged poor intake can become dangerous, especially in overweight cats or cats with existing disease. A cat who walks to the food bowl, sniffs, and leaves may have nausea, dental pain, mouth ulcers, stress, or fever.

Increased appetite can also be a warning sign when paired with weight loss. Common causes include hyperthyroidism, diabetes, intestinal disease, and poor nutrient absorption.

### 4. Drinking or Urinating More

Filling the water bowl more often, seeing larger litter clumps, or cleaning the box more frequently can point to kidney disease, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, urinary disease, or medication effects.

Do not restrict water. Instead, measure what you are seeing and schedule a veterinary exam with bloodwork and urinalysis.

### 5. Weight Loss or Body Shape Changes

Weight loss is one of the biggest missed signs in cats because it can happen slowly under fur. Use a baby scale or weigh yourself holding your cat, then subtract your weight. A small cat losing half a pound can be significant.

If weight loss is your main concern, read [why your cat may be losing weight](/blog/why-is-my-cat-losing-weight) and the [cat weight management guide](/blog/cat-weight-management).

### 6. Grooming Changes

Under-grooming can make the coat look greasy, dandruffy, clumped, or matted. It may signal arthritis, dental pain, obesity, fever, weakness, or chronic illness. Over-grooming one area can point to skin disease, fleas, allergies, pain, or bladder discomfort.

The key signal is a new pattern: a neat cat becoming unkempt, or a relaxed cat creating bald patches.

### 7. Sleeping Position Changes

A cat in pain may rest hunched with paws tucked tightly, head down, eyes half closed, or abdomen guarded. Some cats stop stretching out or avoid lying on one side. Others stop jumping to favorite spots.

One unusual nap is not a diagnosis. A new posture that repeats for more than a day deserves attention.

### 8. Third Eyelid Visible

The third eyelid should usually stay hidden when a cat is awake and alert. If a whitish or pinkish film covers part of the eye, it can happen with dehydration, eye disease, GI upset, systemic illness, or neurologic issues.

If both third eyelids are visible, or if there is squinting, cloudiness, discharge, or pain, schedule care quickly.

### 9. Vocalization Changes

A quiet cat who becomes restless and vocal, especially at night, may be dealing with hyperthyroidism, hypertension, pain, anxiety, cognitive decline, or hearing changes. A vocal cat who suddenly becomes silent can also be unwell.

Record a short video if the behavior happens at home but not at the clinic. That helps your veterinarian see the pattern.

### 10. Bad Breath, Drooling, or Chewing Differently

Bad breath is not normal cat breath. Dental disease is common, but mouth pain can also come from ulcers, resorptive lesions, kidney disease, oral tumors, or injury. Dropping food, chewing on one side, pawing at the mouth, or refusing dry food are clues.

Dental pain can reduce appetite and grooming, so it often looks like "getting old" instead of a mouth problem.

### 11. Vomiting, Hairballs, or Diarrhea

Occasional hairballs can happen, especially in long-haired cats, but frequent "hairballs" that look mostly like fluid or food are vomiting. Repeated vomiting, diarrhea, blood, lethargy, belly pain, or loss of appetite should prompt a call.

If toxin exposure is possible, check the [household poison guide](/blog/common-household-poisons-pets) and call for professional advice.

### 12. Breathing Changes

Open-mouth breathing, panting at rest, noisy breathing, neck extension, blue or pale gums, or obvious belly effort are emergencies. A sleeping respiratory rate that is consistently high can also indicate heart or lung disease.

Count breaths when your cat is asleep: one rise and fall of the chest equals one breath. Share the number with your vet if it seems elevated or has changed from baseline.

## What to Track Before Calling the Vet

Good notes can shorten the path to a diagnosis. Write down:

- When the change started.
- What your cat ate and drank in the last 24 hours.
- Urine and stool frequency.
- Vomiting or diarrhea count, with photos if useful.
- Current medications, supplements, flea products, or recent diet changes.
- Any possible toxin, plant, string, medication, or cleaning-product exposure.
- Weight trend if you have one.
- Short videos of breathing, walking, limping, vocalizing, or litter box behavior.

If the visit may become urgent, prepare with the [pet emergency kit checklist](/blog/pet-emergency-kit-checklist) and review likely [emergency vet costs](/insurance/emergency-vet-cost).

## When Is It an Emergency?

Go to the emergency vet immediately if your cat:

- Is straining to urinate and producing little or no urine.
- Has open-mouth breathing, labored breathing, blue gums, or collapse.
- Is unable to stand, unresponsive, or having seizures.
- Has pale, white, blue, brown, or very tacky gums.
- Has repeated vomiting or diarrhea with weakness, blood, or dehydration.
- Has swallowed a known toxin, lily, antifreeze, medication, string, or sharp object.
- Has sudden paralysis, severe pain, or a rapidly swollen abdomen.

Trust pattern recognition. You know your cat's normal habits better than anyone. If something is clearly different and getting worse, calling your veterinarian is the right next step.
