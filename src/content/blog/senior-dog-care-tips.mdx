## Quick Answer: Senior Dog Care

Senior dog care is about catching small changes early: schedule twice-yearly veterinary exams, track weight and appetite, adjust exercise before joints flare, keep dental disease under control, and watch for behavior changes that may mean pain, sensory decline, or cognitive dysfunction. A dog is "senior" earlier if they are large or giant breed, so do not wait for obvious frailty before changing the care plan.

## When Is a Dog "Senior"?

It depends on breed size. Small dogs age more slowly than large dogs:

- **Small breeds under 20 lbs**: senior at 10–12 years
- **Medium breeds (20 to 50 lbs)**: senior at 8–10 years
- **Large breeds (50 to 90 lbs)**: senior at 7–9 years
- **Giant breeds over 90 lbs**: senior at 6–7 years

A Great Dane at 7 has lived the equivalent of a Chihuahua at 14. This matters because large-breed dogs need earlier screening for age-related conditions.

## 1. Adjust the Diet

Senior dogs typically need fewer calories — their metabolism slows and they're less active. But their protein needs actually increase to combat age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia).

**Senior diet priorities:**
- **Higher protein**: 30–40% on a dry matter basis to preserve lean mass
- **Moderate fat**: 10–15% — enough for palatability and energy, but not obesity-promoting
- **Omega-3 fatty acids**: EPA and DHA from fish oil support joint health and cognitive function
- **Joint supplements**: Glucosamine and chondroitin have moderate evidence for osteoarthritis support
- **Phosphorus moderation**: In dogs with early kidney disease, controlled phosphorus is important — but only if bloodwork indicates it's needed

## 2. Manage Joint Pain and Arthritis

80% of senior dogs have radiographic evidence of arthritis. The signs are subtle: slowing down on walks, hesitating before stairs, difficulty rising, "bunny hopping" with hind legs, or behavioral changes like irritability when touched.

**Evidence-based management:**
1. **Weight control above all else**: Every extra pound adds ~4 pounds of pressure on joints
2. **Joint supplements**: Dasuquin, Cosequin, or similar — effect takes 4–6 weeks
3. **Adequan injections**: Disease-modifying OA drug that supports cartilage repair. Requires vet administration
4. **NSAIDs prescribed by your vet**: Carprofen, meloxicam, and others. Never use human NSAIDs — they cause kidney failure in dogs
5. **Librela (bedinvetmab)**: Monthly monoclonal antibody injection targeting NGF — newer option with strong OA data
6. **Physical therapy and hydrotherapy**: Underwater treadmill reduces joint loading while building muscle

## 3. Watch for Cognitive Decline

Canine Cognitive Dysfunction (CCD) — essentially dog dementia — affects approximately 28% of dogs aged 11–12 and 68% of dogs aged 15–16.

**Signs (acronym DISHA):**
- **D**isorientation: staring at walls, getting stuck in corners, not recognizing familiar people
- **I**nteraction changes: less interest in greeting, more clingy or more withdrawn
- **S**leep-wake cycle disruption: pacing at night, sleeping all day
- **H**ouse soiling: accidents in a previously house-trained dog
- **A**ctivity changes: repetitive behaviors, reduced exploration, increased anxiety

**Management**: Selegiline (Anipryl) is FDA-approved for CCD. Environmental enrichment, consistent routines, and diets enriched with antioxidants and medium-chain triglycerides also show benefit.

## 4–8: The Quick List

- **Increase veterinary screening**: Senior dogs need exams every 6 months (not annually). Bloodwork (CBC, chemistry, thyroid), urinalysis, and blood pressure at minimum annually
- **Modify exercise**: Shorter, more frequent walks instead of one long walk. Swimming is ideal. Stop before fatigue, not after
- **Address sensory decline**: Approach from the front if hearing-impaired. Use nightlights for failing vision. Keep furniture layout consistent
- **Dental health**: Senior dogs are at highest risk for dental disease. Anesthesia risk increases with age but modern protocols with pre-anesthetic bloodwork are very safe
- **Keep them mentally engaged**: Food puzzles, short training sessions, new walking routes. The aging brain benefits from novelty

## Senior Dog Checkup Schedule

For healthy adult dogs, an annual visit may be enough. For senior dogs, a six-month rhythm is more useful because a year is a long time in an aging body. At each visit, ask your veterinarian about:

- Body condition score and muscle condition score
- Dental pain, tartar, loose teeth, and oral masses
- Arthritis screening and pain scoring
- Bloodwork, urinalysis, and blood pressure
- Thyroid screening when signs fit
- Lumps, skin changes, and new coughs
- Medication review and supplement safety
- Vision, hearing, and behavior changes

Weight trends are especially useful because slow muscle loss can hide under a stable number on the scale. Pair a home scale or monthly weigh-in with our [dog calorie guide](/blog/calculate-dog-calorie-needs) and [body condition score guide](/blog/understanding-body-condition-score).

## Feeding a Senior Dog Without Guessing

Senior dogs do not all need a "senior food." Some need fewer calories, some need more protein, some need kidney-friendly adjustments, and some need therapeutic diets prescribed after bloodwork. The right plan depends on body condition, muscle condition, dental comfort, kidney values, stool quality, appetite, and disease history.

Use these checkpoints:

- If your dog is gaining fat, measure food by grams instead of scoops.
- If your dog is losing muscle, ask about protein intake and underlying disease.
- If chewing is harder, check teeth before switching foods.
- If appetite drops suddenly, treat it as a medical sign, not aging.
- If your dog has kidney, heart, urinary, or digestive disease, ask before changing diet.

For overweight senior dogs, small calorie cuts are safer than aggressive restriction. For thin senior dogs, unexplained weight loss needs a veterinary workup before simply adding treats.

## Arthritis: Make the House Easier

Senior dogs often show arthritis through behavior rather than obvious limping. They may hesitate at stairs, stand slowly, avoid slippery floors, pant at night, stop jumping into the car, or become irritable when touched.

Home changes can reduce daily pain:

- Use rugs or runners on slick floors.
- Add ramps or steps for furniture and cars.
- Keep nails trimmed so paws grip better.
- Choose short, frequent walks instead of weekend marathons.
- Ask about pain medication rather than relying only on supplements.

Exercise still matters. The goal is controlled movement, not exhaustion. Our [dog exercise by breed guide](/blog/dog-exercise-needs-by-breed) can help you adjust activity for age, breed, and joint risk.

## Dental Disease Is Not Just Bad Breath

Senior dogs commonly have periodontal disease, broken teeth, oral pain, and tooth root infections. Bad breath, drooling, dropping food, chewing on one side, or facial swelling deserve a dental exam.

Home brushing and VOHC-accepted chews help prevention, but they do not fix advanced disease below the gumline. Read the full [dog dental care guide](/blog/dog-dental-care-guide) if your senior dog has tartar, bleeding gums, or trouble chewing.

## Emergency and Insurance Planning for Older Dogs

Senior dogs are more likely to need diagnostics, medications, dental procedures, imaging, or emergency care. Pet insurance is harder to evaluate for older dogs because premiums are higher and pre-existing condition exclusions matter more.

If you are comparing coverage, focus less on the cheapest monthly price and more on whether the plan covers diagnostics, hospitalization, medications, dental injury or disease, cancer, orthopedic conditions, and specialist care. ## Senior Dog Care FAQ

### How often should a senior dog see the vet?

Many senior dogs benefit from veterinary exams every six months. More frequent visits may be needed for dogs with arthritis, dental disease, kidney disease, heart disease, diabetes, cancer, or unexplained weight changes.

### What are common signs of pain in senior dogs?

Pain may show as slowing down, reluctance to climb stairs, panting at night, irritability, licking joints, trouble rising, reduced appetite, or sudden behavior changes. Limping is not the only sign.

### Should senior dogs eat senior dog food?

Not always. Some senior foods help with calories or fiber, but the best diet depends on body condition, muscle loss, dental health, kidney values, and disease history. Ask your veterinarian before switching if your dog has medical issues.

### Is pet insurance worth it for a senior dog?

It can be, but compare carefully. Premiums are usually higher, and pre-existing condition rules matter. Review diagnostic, medication, dental, orthopedic, cancer, and emergency coverage before choosing.
